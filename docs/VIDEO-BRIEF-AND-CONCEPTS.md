# One Interiors — intro film, restart

Two parts: an honest post-mortem on why the first attempt is mediocre, and a brief you can
paste into ChatGPT (or any creative) to get concepts worth making. My own concepts are at
the end — use them as a benchmark for whatever comes back.

---

## Part 1 · Why the first film isn't good

I built most of it, so this is not a dig at the footage.

**1. The structure is the default template.** Problem → hero → process → payoff is what
every explainer video does. Nothing about that shape is specific to One Interiors. A viewer
has seen this exact film for a dozen other products.

**2. The voiceover lists features instead of making an argument.** "OneQuiz lijiye, OneQuote
se quotation paaiye, OneCompare se compare kijiye" is four product names in ten seconds. It
tells you what the buttons are called. It does not tell you why you should trust anyone.

**3. "Best of the best" is the weakest sentence in the script**, and it sits at the exact
moment the film is supposed to earn credibility. Everyone claims it. It reads as noise.

**4. The live action is generic by construction.** A couple in an empty flat is the stock
image of this entire category. Well executed — but a viewer cannot tell it from a MagicBricks
ad, a Livspace ad, or a paint commercial.

**5. And the real asset is buried.** The single most distinctive thing about this product
appears for about two seconds, three-quarters of the way in:

> Terra Firma Interiors · **+9 DAYS OVER PROMISE**
> 12 of 15 verified · **3 not checked yet**
> *"No studio can pay to change any of this."*

**You publish the numbers that make your own studios look bad.** No competitor in Indian
interiors does that, and none of them can start without rebuilding their business. That is
the film. Everything else is packaging.

## Part 2 · The brief — paste this into ChatGPT

> I need concepts for a 45-second intro film for a startup. Read everything below, then
> give me **five genuinely different concepts** — not five variations on one idea. For each,
> give me the core idea in one sentence, the opening three seconds, a beat-by-beat outline
> with timings, and the closing line. Then tell me which one you'd bet on and why.
>
> **The company.** One Interiors, launching in Pune, India. A marketplace that matches
> homeowners with verified interior design studios. Currently pre-launch, collecting a
> waitlist.
>
> **What the category is like.** Indian interior design is a low-trust market. The standard
> horror story is real: you get three wildly different quotes for the same kitchen, the
> budget inflates 40% mid-project, the designer stops answering, and the job runs months
> late. Competitors (Livspace, HomeLane, MagicBricks Interiors) all advertise the same
> things — beautiful finished rooms, "trusted partners", "5-star designers", EMI offers.
> Every ad in this category is lifestyle photography plus a discount.
>
> **What makes us actually different — this is the important part.** We run 15 independent
> verification checks on every studio, each with a named external source and a date:
> PAN name match (Protean), principal's identity (IDFY), GST registration and 12 months of
> filings (GST Portal), company filings (MCA), Udyam/MSME registration, litigation and
> consumer-forum search (eCourts + NCDRC), plus physical checks our own team does — address
> visited, past clients contacted, completed sites inspected.
>
> And we publish the results honestly, including the unflattering ones. On our live product
> a studio's card can read "12 of 15 verified · 3 not checked yet" and "+9 days over
> promise." We show delivered project counts and average overrun. Our line is: *"No studio
> can pay to change any of this."*
>
> Nobody else in this market publishes their own bad numbers. We think that is the whole
> argument, and our first attempt at a film buried it.
>
> **The offer.** Join the waitlist, get a free consultation with a certified architect who
> reviews your plans before you commit to anything.
>
> **The audience.** Urban Indian couples, 28–40, who have just taken possession of a 2 or
> 3 BHK flat in Pune. They are excited and frightened. This is often the largest discretionary
> purchase of their life after the flat itself. They are researching on Instagram and
> YouTube, and they talk in a mix of Hindi and English.
>
> **Constraints.** 45 seconds for the website, plus a 20-second cut for Instagram and
> YouTube pre-roll. Hindi voiceover with English product terms. Small budget. We can
> generate AI video (Higgsfield/Kling), animate real UI, screen-record our real product,
> and do simple motion graphics — but we cannot shoot a crew, cast actors, or licence music
> expensively. AI video cannot render legible text or interfaces, so anything that depends
> on readable numbers has to be motion graphics or a real screen recording.
>
> **What we already tried and why it failed.** A four-scene film: stressed couple in an
> empty flat → lineup of verified designers → product walkthrough → happy couple with an
> architect on a tablet. It is competent and completely generic. The voiceover lists product
> features. The transparency angle appears for two seconds near the end.
>
> **What I want from you.** Concepts where the *idea* does the work, not the production
> value — because we can't outspend anyone. At least two of the five should use no actors
> at all. At least one should be uncomfortable or risky. Tell me honestly if any concept
> would be hard to execute with the constraints above.

## Part 3 · My five concepts, as a benchmark

Whatever ChatGPT returns, compare it against these. If its ideas are weaker, push back with
"be more specific to the transparency angle."

### A · "Nine"  ★ my pick

Black screen. A number fades up, very large: **+9**

> *"Nau din. Ek studio humare apne platform par — nau din late tha."*
> (Nine days. A studio on our own platform — was nine days late.)

Beat. Then the reveal: it's a real card from the real product, with the overrun printed on it.

> *"Humne yeh chhupaya nahi. Humne chhaapa."* (We didn't hide it. We printed it.)

The rest of the film is the argument that follows: a marketplace that publishes its own bad
numbers is the only one you can believe about the good ones.

> **Closing:** *"Har doosri jagah aapko paanch sitaare dikhayegi. Hum aapko nau dikhate hain."*
> (Everywhere else will show you five stars. We show you the nine.)

**Why it works:** it opens on a confession, which is the last thing anyone expects from an
ad. It is unfakeable — a competitor would have to publish their own failures to copy it.
And it needs no actors: one number, real product cards, typography.

**Risk:** your team will be nervous about opening on a negative. That nervousness is the
signal it's working.

### B · "Fifteen"

No people. No rooms. Pure typography and data, cut to a rhythm. Fifteen checks count off on
screen, each stamped with its real source and date — PROTEAN, IDFY, GST PORTAL, MCA, UDYAM,
ECOURTS + NCDRC. Then the counter stops at twelve.

> *"Baaki teen? Abhi tak check nahi hue. Woh bhi likha hai."*
> (The other three? Not checked yet. That's written down too.)

**Why it works:** every competitor's ad is a beautiful room. An ad made of government
registries and dates is unmissable purely by contrast — and it is the proof, not a claim
about the proof.

### C · "Same kitchen, three prices"

Three quotations for an identical 2BHK kitchen, side by side, totals counting up to three
very different numbers. The viewer's own memory does the work — most of them have lived this.

> *"Ek hi kitchen. Teen daam. Kaunsa sach tha?"* (One kitchen. Three prices. Which was true?)

Then one itemised quote, every line visible, and the architect review before you sign.

**Why it works:** it opens inside the exact anxiety the audience already has, rather than
explaining it to them. Motion graphics only — no actors.

### D · "What we couldn't verify"

The whole film is the gaps. Rate card not filed. Warranty not on paper. Labour insurance
unconfirmed. Days over — unmeasured.

> *"Yeh woh hai jo hum abhi tak verify nahi kar paaye. Hum phir bhi dikha rahe hain."*
> (This is what we haven't been able to verify yet. We're showing it anyway.)

**Why it works:** advertising a product by its holes is close to unheard of, which makes it
memorable and enormously trust-building. Highest risk, highest ceiling.

### E · "The last person before you sign"

One continuous scene: an architect reading someone's plans, finding a problem, and saying so.
No marketplace, no features. Just the moment before the money is committed.

> **Closing:** *"Waitlist par, yeh nazar bilkul free hai."* (On the waitlist, this pair of eyes is free.)

**Why it works:** it sells the single scariest moment in the journey. Also the most
conventional idea here, which makes it the safe fallback.

## Part 4 · What I'd actually change regardless of concept

**Make two films, not one.** A 20-second hook for Instagram and pre-roll, where most people
will meet you, and a 60-second version for the site where someone has already chosen to
watch. Cut both from the same assets.

**Drop "best of the best."** Replace it with a number. "Pandrah checks" beats any superlative
because it can be checked.

**Move the free consultation to the first ten seconds.** It is your strongest offer and it
currently arrives at 0:36, which most viewers never reach.

**Lead with the product, not with people.** Your UI carries the argument — the scores, the
checks, the overrun. AI-generated couples are the one part a competitor can trivially copy.

**Keep the film honest about what it is.** Do not show a finished room implying it is your
work until you have delivered projects and can show real ones. The moment you can, real
before-and-afters from Pune homes will beat every idea on this page.

---

## Part 5 · If you want to test before committing

Cut three 15-second versions — A, B and C — using stills and motion graphics only. No
Higgsfield, no voice artist. A few hours of work.

Run them as Instagram ads to Pune, ₹1,500 each. Watch the three-second hold rate and the
cost per waitlist signup.

That data is worth more than any opinion in this document, including mine. Then make the
real film out of whichever one people actually watched.
